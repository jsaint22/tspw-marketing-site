# The Jock Tax Is the Most Expensive Thing Nobody Talks to Hockey Players About

You play 84 regular-season games starting in 2026-27. Half at home, half on the road. Many of those road games create a tax obligation where the puck dropped — though not all of them, which is the first thing most players are told wrong.

Welcome to the jock tax. It's the most expensive financial reality in professional hockey, and almost nobody is talking to players about it.

## What the Jock Tax Actually Is

The jock tax is the colloquial name for state and local income taxes levied on visiting professional athletes. The concept is straightforward: if you earn income in a state, that state has the right to tax it — even if you don't live there and were only there for one game.

For a hockey player, this means a portion of your salary is subject to taxation in every state (and Canadian province) where you play a game. The standard allocation method divides your total compensation by the number of "duty days" — which includes game days, practice days, and travel days — and assigns income to each jurisdiction based on how many duty days you spent there.

If you're on the road for 100 duty days across the 13 states that actually tax visiting athletes, each of those states gets a slice of your income based on how many of those 100 days were spent within their borders. Road games in Florida, Texas, Tennessee, Nevada and Washington don't create a state tax bill at all.

It sounds manageable. It's not.

## How It Works in Practice

Let's walk through a real scenario.

A player earns $4 million in base salary. They have approximately 200 duty days in a season. Each duty day represents $20,000 in allocated daily income.

Now let's say this player's team travels to New York for a two-game road trip. That's roughly four duty days (travel day, practice day, two game days). Four duty days at $20,000 each means $80,000 of income allocated to New York state.

That $80,000 is not simply multiplied by New York's headline rate. New York computes the tax as if the player were a resident, then apportions it by the New York share of duty days. And the headline rate is not the one that applies: 10.9% is New York's top bracket and does not begin until $25,000,000 of income. A player at $4 million sits in the **9.65%** bracket.

There is no New York **City** tax to add. NYC's nonresident earnings tax was repealed on 1 July 1999, and it has not applied to visiting players since.

Now multiply the exercise by every road trip. California, New Jersey and Minnesota all tax the allocated income. Ontario, Quebec, Alberta, British Columbia and Manitoba bring Canadian rules.

Most NHL players have a filing nexus in roughly 19 to 25 jurisdictions per season — about 13 states, 5 cities with their own income tax, and Canada, The count moves with cross-border travel, postseason play and mid-season trades rather than with division alignment — every team visits most of the league under the current schedule. Each with different rules, different rates, different filing requirements, and different deadlines.

## State-by-State Reality for NHL Cities

Here's the landscape across the NHL. **Rates below were verified in September 2026 and change every year** — several changed in 2026 alone. They are planning context, not filing guidance, and anything you are about to act on should be re-checked against the state's own current guidance.

### High-Tax States (Top Marginal Rate Above 8%)

**California (Anaheim, Los Angeles, San Jose):** graduated, topping out at 13.3% — a 12.3% bracket plus a 1% Behavioral Health Services Tax (renamed from the Mental Health Services Tax in 2025) on income above $1 million. The 13.3% applies only to income above the top threshold, not to a whole salary. California also has no reciprocity agreements, and it audits departed residents closely.

**New York (Rangers, Islanders, Buffalo):** graduated to 10.9%, but that top bracket starts at $25,000,000 — a $4M player is in the 9.65% bracket. **There is no New York City tax on visiting players**; the nonresident earnings tax was repealed in 1999. And the Islanders play at UBS Arena in **Elmont, Nassau County — outside New York City** in any case.

**New Jersey (Devils):** graduated to 10.75%, which reaches income above $1 million. Newark's own payroll tax is levied on employers, not on visiting players.

**Minnesota (Minnesota Wild):** 9.85% top rate. A division rival for many Western Conference teams, meaning multiple road trips per season.

### Medium-Tax States (5% to 8%)

**Massachusetts (Bruins):** 5%, **plus a 4% surtax on income above roughly $1.1 million** — so 9% at the margin for most NHL salaries. Calling Massachusetts a flat 5% state understates it badly for this population.

**Colorado (Colorado Avalanche):** 4.4% flat rate. Lower than many peers.

**Michigan (Red Wings):** 4.25% flat at state level, plus a Detroit city income tax on nonresidents. *(The Detroit municipal rate is not verified here — confirm before relying on it.)*

**Pennsylvania (Penguins, Flyers):** 3.07% flat, plus two city taxes. **Philadelphia** levies a nonresident wage tax that steps down each 1 July, so any single calendar year straddles two rates. **Pittsburgh** levies a 1% earned income tax on nonresident athletes — its former 3% facility-usage fee was struck down under the state Uniformity Clause and the ordinary earned-income tax applies instead.

**Ohio (Blue Jackets):** state rate **2.75%**, plus a Columbus municipal income tax on top. *(The Columbus municipal rate is not verified here — confirm before relying on it.)*

**North Carolina (Hurricanes):** **3.99%** flat.

**Utah (Utah Hockey Club):** **4.45%** flat. The franchise relocated from Arizona in 2024 — **Arizona no longer hosts an NHL team**, so it is not a jock-tax jurisdiction for the NHL at all.

### Low-Tax or No-Tax States

**Florida (Florida Panthers, Tampa Bay Lightning):** No state income tax. This is why Florida residency is a legitimate planning choice for players — not a loophole. What it is worth depends entirely on the contract and the schedule.

**Texas (Dallas Stars):** No state income tax.

**Nevada (Vegas Golden Knights):** No state income tax.

**Washington (Seattle Kraken):** No state income tax.

**Tennessee (Nashville Predators):** No state income tax on earned income.

### Canadian Provinces

**Ontario (Ottawa Senators, Toronto Maple Leafs):** Combined federal and provincial rates can exceed 53% at top marginal brackets. Canadian tax obligations add US-Canada treaty complexity for American players and vice versa.

**Quebec (Canadiens):** combined federal and provincial rates reach roughly 53.3% at the top margin — among the highest in the league. **Quebec is also the only province requiring a separate provincial return**; the other four settle through one federal filing.

**Alberta (Calgary Flames, Edmonton Oilers):** Lower provincial rates than Ontario and Quebec, but still subject to Canadian federal rates.

**British Columbia (Vancouver Canucks):** Combined rates can reach approximately 53.5% at top brackets.

**Manitoba (Winnipeg Jets):** Combined rates around 50.4%.

## What It Costs When Nobody's Paying Attention

The jock tax itself isn't the problem. The problem is when nobody is coordinating it.

Here's what happens when a player has a generic financial advisor and a generalist CPA:

**Home state return gets filed. Road state returns get forgotten or filed late.** Many states impose penalties and interest for late filing. A player who misses the filing deadline in six states can accumulate thousands in unnecessary penalties.

**Tax credits aren't properly claimed.** Most states offer a credit for taxes paid to other states — meaning if you pay New York taxes on income earned there, your home state should give you a credit so you're not double-taxed. But claiming those credits requires properly tracking every dollar of income allocated to each jurisdiction and filing in the correct order. A generalist CPA often misses credits, resulting in double taxation.

**Residency planning doesn't happen.** A player who is a California resident is taxed by California on worldwide income, including the hockey salary. California's rates are graduated and top out at 13.3%, which applies only to income above the top threshold — not to the whole salary. Establishing residency in a state with no income tax removes that layer entirely, and it is usually the largest single lever available. But it has to be genuine: California in particular audits departed residents aggressively, and a change on paper without a change in life does not survive that.

**The duty-day allocation is accepted without checking.** Duty days are the standard in the United States, and the main alternative is gone: the Ohio Supreme Court struck down Cleveland's games-played method in *Hillenmeyer v. Cleveland* (2015) as a violation of due process. That means there is no method to shop for — but there is still arithmetic to check. Duty-day counts are built from the schedule, training camp, travel days and rehab time, and a count that is wrong in a jurisdiction's favour is a bill you did not owe. The work is verifying the inputs, not choosing a formula.

**Nobody models the full multi-state picture.** Each state return is prepared in isolation. Nobody is looking at the complete map of obligations, credits, and residency implications to identify the optimal strategy. The result is patchwork compliance instead of coordinated planning.

## What It Costs

There is a real number here, and it is specific to you — which is exactly why this page will not invent one.

What drives it:

- **Where you are a resident.** This is the largest single lever, and it is the one most within your control. A state with no income tax does not tax your salary; a high-rate state taxes all of it that isn't allocated elsewhere.
- **Which states you play in, and how many duty days you spend there.** Road games in Florida, Texas, Tennessee, Nevada and Washington create no state tax. Games in California, New York or Minnesota do.
- **Whether the credits actually get claimed.** A resident state generally credits tax paid to another state. Returns prepared in isolation, by different preparers, are where those credits go missing.
- **When your signing bonus is paid, and how it is drafted.** Whether a bonus is allocated to one year or spread across the contract depends on the contract language — and that is decided before you sign, not at tax time.

**What we will not do is quote you a savings figure on a web page.** Any number that fits every reader fits none of them, and a firm that leads with one is selling a result it cannot know. The honest version is that the levers above are worth running properly, and what they are worth depends on your contract, your residency and your schedule.



## What Addressing It Actually Looks Like

Proper jock tax planning isn't a single action. It's an ongoing coordination process that touches residency, filing, investment, and career decisions.

**Step 1: Residency audit.** Where does the player currently live during the offseason? Is that the optimal tax jurisdiction? What would need to change to establish defensible residency in a no-tax state? This isn't just about changing a mailing address — it requires genuine ties to the new state: driver's license, voter registration, banking relationships, and physical presence.

**Step 2: Multi-state filing infrastructure.** A qualified CPA or tax advisor who specializes in multi-state athlete taxation prepares returns in every required jurisdiction, coordinates credits, and ensures compliance. This isn't one person's part-time job. It's a core function.

**Step 3: Duty-day tracking.** The player or the financial team maintains an accurate record of every duty day — where it was spent, what it was for, and the corresponding income allocation. This documentation is critical for audits.

**Step 4: Proactive credit coordination.** Each state return is prepared in the optimal order to maximize credits and minimize double taxation. The sequencing matters.

**Step 5: Annual strategy review.** Tax rates change. New teams enter new states. Trades happen. The jock tax strategy has to be reviewed and updated every year based on the actual schedule, any mid-season changes, and any shifts in state tax law.

**Step 6: Integration with overall financial plan.** Jock tax planning doesn't exist in isolation. Residency decisions affect estate planning. Income allocation affects investment timing. Tax savings should be deliberately directed into the player's long-term wealth strategy, not just left in a checking account.

## Why Most Advisors Can't Do This

This level of tax coordination requires specialized knowledge that most financial advisors and CPAs don't possess. It's not in the CFP curriculum. It's not in the standard CPA exam. It's a niche specialty that sits at the intersection of sports law, multi-state taxation, and financial planning.

An advisor who serves 100 general clients and two hockey players isn't going to build the infrastructure to handle jock tax coordination. It's not cost-effective for them. So the hockey player gets generic advice and generic tax preparation, and the jock tax cost just becomes an accepted expense that nobody questions.

It shouldn't be.

The jock tax is the most expensive thing nobody talks to hockey players about. And addressing it — properly, proactively, with a team that understands the mechanics — is one of the highest-value financial planning functions a player can invest in.
